BFloat16 Non-Associativity

BF16 is the format that won deep learning — same exponent range as float32, but only 7 mantissa bits. That trade makes overflow rare and rounding errors common.

FP16 vs BFloat16 — Same width, opposite trade-offs

Both formats pack a number into 16 bits. They split those bits differently — and that single choice changes the entire behavior of the format.

BF16 is literally the top 16 bits of a float32. Its exponent field is identical to float32's, which means anything that fits in float32 fits in bf16 without overflow — no more Inf when you accidentally compute a big number.

The cost: only 7 mantissa bits (vs FP16's 10). That's ~8× coarser rounding at every scale. FP16 trades range for precision; BF16 does the opposite. For deep learning — where gradients span many orders of magnitude but you only need a few significant digits — BF16 is the better trade. For graphics, where every value is roughly in [0, 1], FP16's precision wins.

BF16 — How bits become a number

BFloat16: 1 sign · 8 exponent · 7 mantissa bits.

value = (−1)sign · 2E − 127 · 1.mantissa
E is the 8-bit exponent field read as an unsigned integer (0–255). The 127 is the bias — it shifts the range so E = 01111111 means "no scaling" (20 = 1). That's why exact 1.0 has exponent bits 01111111. Same exponent layout as float32 — just truncate the mantissa.

(big + 1) − big  ≠  (big − big) + 1

BF16's huge range means big = 108 fits comfortably — no overflow. But its 7-bit mantissa means the gap between adjacent representable numbers at that scale is enormous. Adding 1 to 108 doesn't change a single bit.

Inside the BF16 adder — only 8 bits of significand to lose

Floating-point addition can't just add two bit patterns. First it has to align the exponents — shift the smaller number's mantissa right until its exponent matches the larger one's. With BF16's tiny 8-bit significand, anything shifted by more than 7 positions is gone.
