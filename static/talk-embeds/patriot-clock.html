0.1 Is Not a Binary Number

Dhahran, Saudi Arabia — February 25, 1991. A Patriot battery fails to track an incoming Scud, which strikes a US Army barracks. 28 soldiers die, ~100 are injured.
Root cause: the system clock counted time in tenths of a second — and 1/10 cannot be written exactly in binary.

1 · The Broken Number

In binary, 1/10 is the infinite repeating fraction 0.0001100110011001100…₂ — the pattern 1100 repeats forever. The Patriot's weapons-control computer stored it in a 24-bit fixed-point register. Everything past the register edge is simply chopped off.

2 · The Drift

A tiny error is harmless once. But the clock ticks 10 times per second and the error is chopping, not rounding — it always falls the same way, so it never cancels. It just accumulates, for as long as the system stays on. The Dhahran battery had been running for ~100 hours straight.

3 · The Range Gate

To confirm a target, the radar predicts where the Scud will be at the next pulse — position = velocity × elapsed time — and only looks inside a small window around that prediction: the range gate. Feed that formula a drifted clock, and the gate opens in the wrong place. The Scud is real, the radar is fine — but the software looks where the Scud was, finds nothing, and drops the track as a false alarm. Drag the uptime slider above and watch.

4 · The Bitter Timeline

  • Feb 11, 1991Israeli forces, running batteries for long stretches, report the tracking drift to the US Army after 8 hours of continuous operation.
  • Feb 16, 1991Software fix completed. Meanwhile the field workaround is simple: reboot regularly — it resets the tick counter to zero. But no one says how often "regularly" is.
  • Feb 25, 1991Dhahran battery, up ~100 hours, clock off by 0.34 s — the gate is ~half a kilometer off. The Scud is never engaged. 28 dead.
  • Feb 26, 1991The fixed software arrives in Dhahran. One day late.
The final irony: a partial patch had already improved the time conversion in some code paths but not others. Two subsystems computed "the same" timestamp and got different answers — the inconsistency is what shifted the gate. The bug wasn't just imprecision; it was two routines disagreeing about what time it was.
Numbers follow the GAO report (IMTEC-92-26) and D. N. Arnold / M. Mignotte's "disasters attributable to bad numerical computing": per-tick chopping error 0.1 × 2⁻²⁰ ≈ 9.5×10⁻⁸ s, drift after 100 h ≈ 0.3433 s, Scud velocity ≈ 1,676 m/s → gate off by ≈ 573 m (GAO's own table quotes 687 m using a slightly different velocity).
